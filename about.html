My Story

From Engineering to Habit Engineering

I've always approached improvement as an engineering problem: understand the system, find what is limiting it, make the important change easier to execute, and measure the result.

At General Motors, I helped develop and lead the Throughput Improvement Process (TIP). One of the reasons it worked so well was something I didn't initially think of as “habit.” We already had a powerful cue: the weekly all-plant leadership meeting. We had a simple process, supported by C-More, which I helped develop: find the constraint and fix what mattered. And the rewards were difficult to ignore—throughput, profit and schedule attainment.

At Detroit/Hamtramck, the larger effort was associated with profit growth from roughly $1 billion to $2 billion and then more than $3 billion, while launch ramp time fell from about six months to six weeks. The broader GM work was later recognized as part of the 2005 INFORMS Franz Edelman Award for Achievement in Advanced Analytics, Operations Research and Management Science.

What struck me later, working around other improvement efforts, was how often the objective was the installation itself. A major DBR implementation could produce a large gain, and the job was considered finished. DDMRP could eliminate shortages and get the software running, and then continuous improvement largely stopped. CCPM and other methods could show excellent initial results without creating a mechanism that kept people improving.

Leadership changes were often blamed when results faded. That explanation never fully satisfied me. GM went through plenty of leadership changes. TIP survived because it did not depend on a particular leader continually pushing it. Leadership could provide the spark, but the process itself supplied the cue, the action and the reward.

By then I had read Charles Duhigg's The Power of Habit, and a pattern that had been hiding in plain sight became obvious. Without deliberately calling it one, we had built a habit.

I started looking at other systems that sustained improvement particularly well. The Toyota Production System provided an obvious example. The andon process, often used as a visible representation of TPS, is not merely a tool. It is a repeated behavior: a cue appears, an action is expected, support follows, the problem is addressed, and the system reinforces doing it again.

That led to a deeper question. It wasn't enough to tell organizations, “You need better habits.” I needed to understand why some improvement behaviors became self-reinforcing while others lost to the old routine—especially when the old behavior was tied to status, experience, incentives or a leader's professional identity.

That question became Habit Engineering: a practical way to connect system focus, behavior, cues, actions, rewards and reinforcement so that improvement does not end with the installation.

Today

Writing, Teaching, and Sharing What Works

I'm not building a traditional consulting practice. Today my focus is on writing, teaching and sharing practical ideas through books, classes and this website.

Habit Engineering brings together what I've learned from a career in manufacturing and continuous improvement with what research tells us about how habits are created and sustained.

It is a bridge between proven improvement methods such as the Theory of Constraints and Lean/TPS, and the human behavior that determines whether those improvements remain in place after the initial excitement is gone.

My goal is simple: help people focus on what truly matters, improve it, and create the conditions that make the better behavior easier to repeat.
