If It's a Behavior Problem, It's a Habit Problem.

Training transfers knowledge. Procedures describe what people should do. Metrics tell us what happened. Leadership can demand compliance.

But none of those guarantees that tomorrow—when production falls behind, the supervisor changes, or the improvement champion leaves—people will continue doing what they were taught.

Under pressure, old habits compete with the new process. Old habits have an enormous advantage: they have already been practiced and rewarded.

CUE → ACTION → REWARD → REPEAT

Habit Engineering deliberately designs the conditions that allow better behaviors to become easier and more natural to repeat.

The Habit Engineering Method

Don't try to change everything. Focus improvement where it matters, identify the behaviors necessary to achieve it, and engineer those behaviors into habits.

1

Define the Goal

What result are we trying to achieve?

2

Find the Constraint

What is preventing us from achieving more?

3

Identify the Critical Behavior

What must people repeatedly do differently?

4

Understand the Old Habit

What cues and rewards keep the existing behavior alive?

5

Engineer the New Habit

Create the cue, action and stronger reward.

6

Repeat & Reinforce

Make the new behavior easier to repeat than the old habit.

7

Measure the Result

Did the system actually improve?

When the constraint moves, the focus moves. The habit of improvement remains.

Habit Engineering + Continuous Improvement

Habit Engineering doesn't replace your improvement method. It addresses the behavioral problem that determines whether the method continues working after the installation, event or initial enthusiasm is over.

Theory of Constraints

Find the constraint and focus improvement where it changes system performance. Habit Engineering helps make the behaviors required to exploit and elevate that constraint repeatable.

Lean / TPS

Standard work, visual management, problem solving and Kaizen become powerful when the required behaviors are repeatedly practiced and reinforced.

Continuous Improvement

The specific method can change. The behavioral challenge remains: will people continue using and improving the process after the experts leave?

About Kevin Kohls

Kevin Kohls is an engineer, manufacturing leader and author whose work has centered on a deceptively difficult question: why do some improvements become the normal way of working while others disappear after the initial push?

His experience with throughput improvement at General Motors—and later with Lean/TPS, the Theory of Constraints and other improvement approaches—eventually led to Habit Engineering.
